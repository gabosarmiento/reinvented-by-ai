# Second-Edition Outline

Status: proposed; no chapter drafting begins before author approval  
Recommended length: 48,000–55,000 words excluding worksheets, notes, and bibliography  
Architecture: six parts, eighteen short chapters, preface, conclusion, and a practical field kit

## Structural decision

The six-part direction proposed in the brief is stronger than the original eight-chapter sequence, but it needs shorter chapters and a sharper separation of concerns.

The original structure moves from urgency to a comprehensive ATOM description, then repeats governance, alignment, learning, measurement, and leadership across multiple chapters. The new structure follows the life of an operating system:

1. establish why the constraint moved;
2. diagnose the existing system;
3. define ATOM and its boundaries;
4. show the operating loop in use;
5. build and evaluate a first loop;
6. scale and lead it responsibly.

Each major mechanism gets one definitional home. Later chapters apply it by reference rather than explaining it again.

## Front matter

### Preface — What Changed Between 2025 and 2026

Purpose: Establish the honest provenance of the book and explain why the second edition is a reconstruction of an early hypothesis.

Reader question answered: Why should I trust a revised model that was first proposed speculatively?

Original material reused: The 2025 “day in 2026” premise and the conviction that AI changes the company, not only individual tasks.

Original material removed: Claims that the first edition already described an emerging reality or a proven implementation system.

New material required: Author account of why the first edition was created, which beliefs were provisional, what work since then changed the model, and what remains a hypothesis.

Evidence/research required: A compact 2025–2026 timeline of material agent, evaluation, integration, and governance developments. No trend catalogue.

Case studies/examples: Two short contrasts—one prediction that strengthened and one that failed or became more complicated.

Key model/diagram: “2025 assumption / 2026 observation / second-edition consequence” table.

Expected word count: 1,500.

Takeaway: The author is not defending an old forecast; he is exposing how an operating-model hypothesis matured.

Relationship to previous chapter: Opens the book.

Relationship to next chapter: Establishes the evidence standard for the shift described in Chapter 1.

# Part I — The Constraint Moved

## Chapter 1 — When Production Gets Cheap

Purpose: Show how falling cost and latency for some cognitive and software tasks move the bottleneck from production to choosing, contextualizing, integrating, reviewing, and governing work.

Reader question answered: What has actually changed enough to justify a new operating model?

Original material reused: The intuition that adding headcount and software is no longer the default route to scale; the concern about planning latency.

Original material removed: “One AI year equals ten business years,” “15 AI days,” the wave metaphor, and universal declarations of obsolescence.

New material required: A constraint-based argument; distinction between output, throughput, outcome, and value; cheap generation versus expensive integration; the uneven economics of different tasks.

Evidence/research required: Inference-cost trends; agent capability horizons and caveats; field evidence on customer support, consulting, coding, and coordination; total system cost.

Case studies/examples: **REAL CASE** contrasting measured customer-support gains with measured experienced-developer slowdown; **ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE** of a team generating ten prototypes but lacking decision capacity to select one.

Key model/diagram: “The moving constraint” chain: generate → decide → integrate → verify → operate.

Expected word count: 2,600.

Takeaway: More production capacity creates value only if the surrounding operating system can absorb it.

Relationship to previous chapter: Turns the preface’s historical reflection into a causal argument.

Relationship to next chapter: If the constraint moved, the reader needs a way to see where the old system now accumulates debt.

## Chapter 2 — Hidden Operational Debt

Purpose: Turn the book’s most distinctive diagnosis into a precise, usable concept.

Reader question answered: Why does a company feel busier and faster while becoming harder to change?

Original material reused: Meeting load, duplicate work, lost decisions, approval delays, workarounds, fragmented communication, and compounding friction.

Original material removed: The “silent killer” rhetoric, universal existential claims, and the iceberg as the complete model.

New material required: Definition of debt principal and interest; taxonomy of context, decision, coordination, integration, knowledge, and control debt; how AI repays or creates each type; debt register.

Evidence/research required: Literature on coordination costs, technical/organizational debt, decision latency, change failure, and AI-generated rework. Evidence should inform the model without erasing the author’s term.

Case studies/examples: **AUTHOR EXPERIENCE** required: one transformation in which a visible delivery problem was caused by a hidden operating liability. **ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE** showing a fast coding agent increasing review and integration debt.

Key model/diagram: Operational Debt Ledger: liability → recurring interest → risk exposure → repayment experiment.

Expected word count: 3,000.

Takeaway: The first task is not to add AI; it is to locate the liabilities that consume the organization’s new capacity.

Relationship to previous chapter: Names the organizational drag exposed when production speeds up.

Relationship to next chapter: Creates the diagnostic basis for distinguishing tool adoption from operating-model change.

## Chapter 3 — Why Tools Do Not Change the Operating Model

Purpose: Demonstrate why local adoption can raise output without changing interdependent work.

Reader question answered: Why are our pilots impressive while the company remains slow?

Original material reused: The warning against layering software on fragmented processes and the critique of superficial transformation.

Original material removed: Vendor catalogues, claims that one unified stack removes silos, and blanket attacks on Agile.

New material required: Define operating model; local versus system optimization; individual versus coordinated work; automation of a bad handoff; the “paved cow path” failure.

Evidence/research required: Field evidence showing that independently changeable behaviors improve before collaborative routines; adoption and productivity evidence with limitations.

Case studies/examples: **REAL CASE** from the 6,000-worker Microsoft randomized field experiment: less email time but no significant meeting-time change. **ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE** of automating a three-stage approval process without changing its decision rights.

Key model/diagram: Tool layer versus operating-system layers.

Expected word count: 2,400.

Takeaway: A new tool changes a task; an operating-model change alters the interfaces among intent, authority, work, and evidence.

Relationship to previous chapter: Shows how naïve adoption can add operational debt.

Relationship to next chapter: Clears the ground for a bounded definition of ATOM.

# Part II — The ATOM Model

## Chapter 4 — ATOM: Intent In, Evidence Out

Purpose: Give the book one canonical, drawable architecture and define its boundary.

Reader question answered: What exactly is ATOM?

Original material reused: Strategy-to-execution flow, human-agent work, observability, learning, guardrails, and the operating-system metaphor.

Original material removed: The seven mixed “pillars,” AI-as-oxygen language, infinite scalability, and ATOM as a software platform.

New material required: Six layers—Intent, Context, Decisions, Execution, Evidence, Learning—and one cross-cutting control plane; inputs and outputs; human and agent responsibilities; explicit non-goals.

Evidence/research required: Operating-model and control-system foundations; comparison with Lean, Agile, platform operating models, Team Topologies, sociotechnical systems, and AI governance frameworks. The comparison must identify inheritance and difference, not perform a literature parade.

Case studies/examples: **ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE** mapping one customer-refund or product-change decision through all layers.

Key model/diagram: Canonical ATOM architecture. This diagram becomes the visual grammar for the rest of the book.

Expected word count: 3,200.

Takeaway: ATOM is a governed learning loop, not a dashboard, org chart, or collection of agents.

Relationship to previous chapter: Supplies the system redesign that tool adoption lacks.

Relationship to next chapter: Opens the first layer beneath intent: governed context.

## Chapter 5 — Context Is Infrastructure

Purpose: Explain how agents and humans obtain the right information, history, policy, and state without treating memory as magic.

Reader question answered: What must the organization know—and prove it knows—before an agent can act?

Original material reused: Contextual memory, knowledge graph, live data, and the Playbook as shared knowledge.

Original material removed: Claims that relevant knowledge is automatically available, correct, and current.

New material required: Context products; provenance; freshness; scope; retrieval; access; memory types; contamination; expiration; context ownership; minimum necessary context.

Evidence/research required: Primary documentation and research on context management, retrieval, agent memory, data governance, prompt injection through retrieved content, and provenance.

Case studies/examples: **ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE** in which an agent recommends the wrong supplier because policy and contract context are stale; corrected design with provenance and expiry.

Key model/diagram: Context envelope: task state + enterprise facts + policy + history + provenance + access.

Expected word count: 2,800.

Takeaway: An agent’s useful autonomy is bounded by the quality and authority of its context.

Relationship to previous chapter: Deepens ATOM’s Context layer.

Relationship to next chapter: Context becomes actionable only through an explicit decision architecture.

Evidence/research required: Decision-rights frameworks, automation bias, calibrated uncertainty, human oversight, and accountable delegation.

Case studies/examples: **ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE** comparing a reversible price-test decision with an irreversible employment decision. **REAL CASE** only if documentation exposes actual rights and controls.

Key model/diagram: Decision Rights Matrix and consequence × reversibility autonomy map.

Expected word count: 3,200.

Takeaway: Autonomy is not a property of an agent; it is a grant of authority for a particular decision under particular conditions.

Relationship to previous chapter: Converts governed context into bounded choice.

Relationship to next chapter: Establishes the contract under which humans and agents execute.

## Structure evaluation

Why this is stronger than the original:

- It proves the need before naming the model.
- It gives ATOM one architecture rather than seven mixed pillars.
- It puts decision rights before autonomy.
- It distinguishes running one loop from scaling many.
- It treats governance and evidence as executable mechanisms.
- It preserves Anya but makes the scenario honest.
- It gives implementation three gates: choose, build, prove.
- It ends on accountability, the strongest boundary for a durable 2026 edition.

Why it retains the six-part proposal:

- The shift, model, operation, build, scale, and leadership progression is intellectually sound.
- The proposed outline’s “what would I do on Monday?” requirement becomes the entire Part IV and Field Kit.
- Technology developments appear where they alter the operating model, not in a trend chapter.

Why eighteen short chapters are justified:

- The original chapters are overloaded and repetitive.
- A digital business book benefits from chapters that answer one executive question.
- Each chapter can be drafted and quality-gated independently.
- The structure remains six parts; chapter count is not novelty for its own sake.
